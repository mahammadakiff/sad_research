Section I–II · Background

What is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)?

Large language models used inside organizations need current, organization-specific knowledge that they were not trained on. RAG, introduced by Lewis et al. [1], combines a pre-trained generator with a retriever over a dense vector index of documents, so the model answers from retrieved passages, and its knowledge can be updated by changing the index instead of retraining the model.

1. Question

A user asks a question. It is converted into an embedding vector.

2. Retrieve documents

The retriever returns the top-k most similar chunks from the vector database.

3. Model answers from them

A prompt with the instructions, the question and the retrieved chunks goes to the LLM, which answers, often with links and citations [1], [7].

RAG became the common enterprise pattern

A survey of 600 U.S. enterprise IT decision-makers reported that RAG adoption rose from 31% to 51% between 2023 and 2024, while fine-tuning remained rare [2].

Read with care: the surveying firm invests in companies it reports on [27].

Why this creates a security problem

The model reads text that neither the developer nor the user wrote, from a store that may hold documents with different owners and sensitivity levels, and users trust its answers because they cite official sources. The LLM receives one undivided stream of text:

the LLM “is unable to distinguish which sections of prompt belong to various input sources” [8]
retrieval blurs “the line between data and instructions” [9]

Every document that can reach the index therefore crosses a trust boundary into the model’s context. The 2025 OWASP list added a RAG-specific category, LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses [3].

Section IV

The six risks in detail

Research demonstrates all six risks. For three of them (A4, A5, A6) the paper also found deployed incidents; it found no deployed incident of A1, A2 or A3.

Shown in researchA1, A2, A3 Seen in deployed incidentsA4, A5, A6
A1 Shown in research

Knowledge-base poisoning

The attacker adds crafted texts to the knowledge database so that, for an attacker-chosen question, the texts are retrieved and lead the LLM to an attacker-chosen answer (PoisonedRAG, USENIX Security 2025) [13].

about 90%attack success with five malicious texts per target question in a database of millions of texts [13]

AgentPoison reached an average attack success of at least 80% while poisoning less than 0.1% of the memory or knowledge base, with at most 1% impact on normal performance [19].

OWASP LLM04, LLM08 · MITRE ATLAS AML.T0070 (RAG Poisoning), AML.T0071 (False RAG Entry Injection) [11]

A2 Shown in research

Embedding exposure

Vector stores are sometimes treated as less sensitive because they hold numbers rather than words. Morris et al. showed this assumption does not hold [14].

92%of 32-token inputs reconstructed exactly from their embeddings (Vec2Text) [14]

The method also recovered full names from clinical notes; the authors conclude embeddings should be treated with the same precautions as raw data [14].

OWASP LLM08 (embedding inversion) [3]

A3 Shown in research

Retrieval without access control

If retrieval ranks by similarity alone, any user’s question can pull in a chunk the user could not open in the source system [3].

116 of 250composite prompts made the system reproduce retrieved content verbatim (Enron e-mail corpus, GPT-3.5-turbo) [7]

Answers can also reveal whether a document is in the database: a simple prompt performs membership inference [20]. Slack AI is classified under A4 and A6, not A3, because the retrieved channels were public.

OWASP LLM02, LLM08

A4 Seen in deployed incidents

Indirect prompt injection

The attacker does not talk to the model but places instructions in content the application is likely to retrieve, such as a web page, an e-mail or a shared document [9].

LLM01prompt injection is ranked first in the OWASP list [3]

Processing retrieved prompts can act like arbitrary code execution [9]. Retrieval is the mechanism that delivers the payload: the more sources the index ingests, the more places an attacker can plant one.

Incidents: Slack AI, EchoLeak, ForcedLeak, CamoLeak · NIST AI 100-2 [4]

A5 Seen in deployed incidents

Confabulation despite grounding

RAG reduces but does not remove unsupported answers. The model can misread a passage, merge two passages, or answer although no relevant passage exists.

CAD 650.88fare difference plus interest and fees awarded against Air Canada over its chatbot’s answer [22]

NIST AI 600-1 lists confabulation among twelve risks unique to or made worse by generative AI [10]. For an assistant that speaks for an organization, a wrong answer is a liability.

Incidents: Air Canada, NYC MyCity · OWASP LLM09 Misinformation

A6 Seen in deployed incidents

Exfiltration through rendered output

If the model outputs a link or image whose URL contains retrieved data, the client sends that data to the attacker when it loads the image or the user clicks the link.

CVSS 9.3EchoLeak, described as the first real-world zero-click prompt-injection exploit in a production LLM system [17], [24]

“The injection supplies the instruction, retrieval supplies the secret and rendering delivers it.”

Incidents: Slack AI, EchoLeak, ForcedLeak, CamoLeak · OWASP LLM01, LLM05

Section V · Table III

Real incidents, 2024–2025

Six public cases show three of the risks (A4, A5 and A6) in deployed systems. Each card names the control that could have reduced the risk (Table III).

  1. 2024

  2. Air Canada chatbot

    February 2024 · Moffatt v. Air Canada, decided Feb. 14, 2024 [22]
    A5

    The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal held the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation after its website chatbot told a customer that a bereavement fare could be claimed retroactively, which contradicted the airline’s own policy. The tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity and awarded the fare difference of CAD 650.88 plus interest and fees [22]. The chatbot’s architecture was not disclosed, so the case is weaker evidence for RAG specifically.

    Control: Answers bound to the current policy text, with refusal when none applies

  3. NYC MyCity chatbot

    March 2024 · reported by The Markup, Mar. 29, 2024 [23]
    A5

    New York City’s Microsoft-powered MyCity chatbot told users that employers could take a share of workers’ tips and that landlords could refuse housing vouchers, both illegal in the city. It gave different answers when several staff members asked the same question, and carried a disclaimer that its responses may be inaccurate or incomplete [23]. A disclaimer does not repair an inaccurate answer.

    Control: Grounding check and accuracy testing before release

  4. Slack AI

    August 2024 · disclosed by PromptArmor, Aug. 20, 2024 [21]
    A4A6

    Slack AI retrieved content from public channels even when the querying user was not a member. An attacker could post an instruction in a public channel; when a victim later asked about data in a private channel, the answer drew on both and rendered a link that would send the data, for example an API key, to the attacker [21]. Reading a public channel was within the user’s permissions, so no access control was bypassed.

    Control: Channel content treated as data; no unvetted links in answers
  5. 2025

  6. EchoLeak in Microsoft 365 Copilot

    2025 · reported Jan. 2025, fixed in May, disclosed June 11, 2025 [17] · CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3 [24]
    A4A6

    Aim Labs found that a remote, unauthenticated attacker could exfiltrate data from a victim’s Copilot context by sending a single crafted e-mail, without any user interaction. The exploit chained bypasses: the e-mail evaded the cross-prompt-injection classifier, reference-style links escaped link redaction, and a Microsoft Teams URL-preview endpoint on the allowlist bypassed the content security policy [17]. No known exploitation in the wild.

    Control: External content kept apart from internal data; egress allowlist covering all link forms

  7. ForcedLeak in Salesforce Agentforce

    2025 · reported July 28, disclosed Sep. 25, 2025 · CVSS 9.4 [25]
    A4A6

    Noma Security placed instructions in the description field of a public Web-to-Lead form; when an employee later asked the agent to work on that lead, it sent other leads’ data out through an image URL. The content security policy still trusted an expired domain, which the researchers bought for five dollars [25].

    Control: Untrusted form data marked as data; allowlist free of expired domains

  8. CamoLeak in GitHub Copilot Chat

    2025 · Legit Security, published Sep. 30, 2025 · CVSS 9.6 [26]
    A4A6

    Copilot Chat followed instructions hidden in invisible comments of a pull-request description. Combined with a content-security-policy bypass through GitHub’s own image proxy, this let an attacker extract secrets and source code from a victim’s private repositories. GitHub fixed it by disabling image rendering in Copilot Chat [26].

    Control: Hidden text treated as untrusted; no image rendering through proxies

The attack chain behind the data leaks

In all four disclosed vulnerabilities that enabled data exfiltration, the injection (A4) caused harm only because retrieval could reach sensitive data the attacker could not see and the answer could send data out of the system (A6).

Retrieval reaches sensitive datasupplies the secret

▼
A6

Breaking any link of that chain would have prevented the loss.

Severity of the three 2025 exfiltration flaws

In EchoLeak, ForcedLeak and CamoLeak a trusted or allowlisted domain carried the data out [17], [25], [26].

Three lessons

  1. Attacks combine attack points. A4 caused harm only together with retrieval of sensitive data and A6.
  2. Detection alone is not enough. The classifier-based defenses in EchoLeak failed; the architecture must restrict where data can flow.
  3. Trusted channels amplify harm. In the reliability cases the harm came through the organization’s own trusted channel, which made users more likely to believe a wrong answer.
Section VI-C · Table V

Why single defenses fail

Defenses that look strong in their original evaluation can fail against an adaptive attacker. Nasr et al. tested 12 recent defenses, including spotlighting, prompt sandwiching, PromptGuard and Model Armor, and bypassed most of them with over 90% success, although many had reported near-zero attack success in their original evaluations [18].

Spotlighting, static attacks [8]
< 2%
indirect-injection success, down from above 50% (GPT-family models)
vs.
Spotlighting, adaptive attacks (AgentDojo) [18]
> 95%
attack success

Duplicate-text filtering

Attack success unchanged [13].

Perplexity-based detection

High false-positive rate [13].

Distance threshold

Less leakage, but lower answer quality: a privacy-utility trade-off [7].

These controls are probabilistic, and NIST notes theoretical limits on achieving full adversarial robustness [4]. The design must assume that some injections succeed and limit their effect through the retrieval, output and privilege layers.

Key points per layer

  • L1 Because a few documents can poison an index, ingestion is a security boundary, not only a data-engineering step; controlling who may add content matters more than detecting bad content afterwards [13].
  • L2 The retriever should return only chunks the requesting user may read in the source system; because embeddings can be inverted [14], the vector store needs the same encryption and access restrictions as the source documents.
  • L3 Spotlighting delimits, marks or encodes retrieved text so the model can recognize it as data [8], but it is probabilistic [18].
  • L4 Check a draft answer against the retrieved passages and refuse when none supports it; allow links and images only from approved domains, covering every link syntax and every proxy endpoint [17], [30].
  • L5 An assistant that only answers has a smaller blast radius than one that can send e-mail or change records [3], [17].
  • L6 The OWASP GenAI Red Teaming Guide organizes testing in four areas [12]; RAGAS measures faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance [15].

Layering has a cost

None of the cited studies measures the engineering cost of layering.

  1. A grounding check on every response, such as the Bedrock contextual grounding check [30], can add latency and per-query cost.
  2. Strict grounding thresholds and refusal rules reduce confabulation (A5) but can refuse valid questions whose wording differs from the official text.
  3. Human approval before indexing prevents premature or poisoned updates (A1) but slows the publication of urgent changes.

These trade-offs need to be measured on the deployed system.

Two gaps in the product documentation

Gap 1 · Ingestion governance

Products concentrate on guardrails around the model and on filtering at retrieval; few address ingestion governance, that is, who may add a document and who approves it, although PoisonedRAG shows that poisoning needs only a few texts and little computation [13]. A grounding check measures agreement with the retrieved text, so it cannot detect a poisoned passage that was retrieved.

Gap 2 · Measured effectiveness

Vendor documentation describes features rather than measured effectiveness, so buyers have to test the controls themselves, for example with the red-teaming approach of Section VI-F; the bypass rates reported by Nasr et al. [18] show why vendor-reported detection rates are not enough.

Section VIII

Application to the ADA AI Assistant

The course project designs the ADA AI Assistant, which answers newcomers’ questions about registration, deadlines and student services at ADA University from approved university documents instead of informal messaging groups [5].

Design: still needs validation

The paper includes no experiments of its own; the mitigation model is a synthesis that still needs validation, for example by testing the ADA assistant prototype against the attacks in Table II.

Requirement 1

The assistant must never disclose one student’s data to another person.

Requirement 2

An official date must not reach students before it is approved, a rule motivated by an incident in which a wrong withdrawal deadline was published [5].

Both requirements were gathered in an interview with an academic advisor.

Design per layer

L1 Ingestion

Only document versions uploaded by the owning office and signed by an approver who is not the owner are indexed; date announcements need a second, different approver, and retired versions leave the index.

L2 Retrieval

Each chunk carries a Public, Students or Staff-only label, retrieval filters by the user’s role, and confidential records are never indexed.

L3 Prompt

The student’s question and the retrieved passages are marked as data in the prompt.

L4 Output

Every statement and date in a draft answer must match an approved passage, otherwise the assistant refers the student to the responsible office, and answers may link only to the university’s own domain.

L5 Privilege

The language model has no tools and no write access.

L6 Assurance

A fixed set of injection, leakage and accuracy test questions is run before each release, approval decisions are logged for audit, and every chat opens with a notice that the user is talking to an AI system.

Four release gates

An index or model update is promoted only if:

  1. 1

    No test query retrieves a passage above the user’s role (Public, Students, Staff-only).

  2. 2

    Every rendered link or image outside the university domain is blocked.

  3. 3

    No prompt in the red-team suite changes an official date or policy answer or produces output outside the allowed format.

  4. 4

    RAGAS faithfulness and context-relevance scores [15] are not lower than those of the previous release.

Release flow

Any failed gate blocks the release.

Accepted cost. The grounding check in (L4) adds response time and may refuse some correctly paraphrased questions, and the second approval in (L1) delays urgent date changes, a delay the design accepts in order not to publish a wrong deadline again.
Residual risk. An insider with approval rights could still publish a false document. The two-person rule for date announcements and the audit log are the main controls for that case.
Sections IX–X

Conclusion and limitations

The review supports three conclusions

  1. RAG security is mainly a system-design problem rather than a model problem: in each incident in Section V, a control at retrieval, ingestion or output could have prevented the harm or limited its impact.
  2. The attack points interact, so controls should be chosen to break chains such as injection, retrieval of a secret and exfiltration.
  3. The defenses with the strongest published results, such as spotlighting [8], are probabilistic and were measured on specific models and datasets, so they reduce rather than remove risk; adaptive attacks have already bypassed them [18].

In short

Research demonstrates all six risks; six incidents from 2024–2025 show three of them in deployed systems, often in combination; and the standards and frameworks (OWASP, NIST, MITRE ATLAS) now name them explicitly. They are mitigated by layered controls along the pipeline: governed ingestion, permission-aware retrieval, separation of instructions from data, grounding and output validation, least privilege, and continuous red teaming.

No single layer is sufficient on its own. Future work will measure these controls on the prototype with red-team tests and RAGAS metrics.

Limitations

  • The field is young: most research dates from 2023–2026, and several key results, such as the adaptive-attack study [18], appeared first as preprints and reached peer-reviewed venues only in 2025–2026.
  • Vendor documentation describes features rather than measured effectiveness.
  • Incident reports depend on what researchers and companies chose to disclose.
  • The review is limited to English-language sources.
  • It includes no experiments of its own, so the mitigation model is a synthesis that still needs validation, for example by testing the ADA assistant prototype against the attacks in Table II.
  • The selection was purposive rather than systematic: result counts per query were not recorded and no independent double screening was used, so the review is not exhaustive and complements surveys that follow a full screening protocol [6] (Section III).
